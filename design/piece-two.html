Organic Light

A kinetic flower lamp exploring organic movement and embodiment in an inorganic light form, built almost entirely from recycled aluminum cans.

Built from waste, on purpose. We set ourselves a personal constraint: build the lamp almost entirely from reclaimed material, mainly aluminum cans, rather than buying new. The goal was never a finished commercial product, only to explore how organic movement and aesthetics could live in an everyday light fixture.

Course project, Roskilde University Team of 3 Stack: ESP32 · Servo Motor · NeoPixel LED · 3D Printing

The project began as a way to visualize the length of a workday. It became something else entirely: an exploration of what organic movement itself could feel like.

Lamps are almost always stationary, with a single function: giving light. We wanted to challenge that. Over nine iterations we explored how a lamp could open and close like a flower, powered by a servo motor and lit from within, while sticking to a self-imposed constraint of building it from reused material.

Problem Field

From tracking time to embodying movement

Research Question How can a lamp embody organic functionality and aesthetics?

Our original idea was a lamp that visually communicated how close the workday was to ending, opening when you arrived at the office and closing when you left. As the project progressed, though, we realized we cared less about the office context and more about the organic quality of the motion itself: what it would feel like for light to move the way a plant does. That shift became the real question behind the project.

Theory & Method

Research through design, grounded in embodiment

Instead of chasing a finished product, we treated every prototype as a way to learn about organic movement itself.

We used Research through Design (Bang & Eriksen, 2014) as our methodological frame: the point was never the finished lamp, but what each prototype taught us about organic functionality and aesthetics in an inorganic object. Paul Dourish's concept of embodiment (2001), the idea that something meaningful happens when a phenomenon exists in real time and real space, shaped how we thought about the lamp's presence in a room. Throughout, we used physical sketching (Buxton, 2008), building small, rough versions of a mechanism before committing to it, to explore ideas with our hands rather than only on paper.

The Iterative Process

Nine iterations, from paper petals to a lit succulent

Every material taught us something before it failed: paper couldn't hold a curve, springs couldn't hold their tension, fabric couldn't survive the wind.

1.0 – 2.0 · Paper and springs

We started with a paper lampshade, cutting fringe into colored paper to let it bend into a flower-like shape. The paper couldn't hold up: it creased, jammed, and wouldn't spring back. Iteration 2.0 swapped in real springs, tensioned as a ring passed over them, but keeping them aligned proved just as hard. Stability became the priority for every iteration after this.

3.0 – 3.2 · Finding the mechanism

This is where the core mechanism was born: a ring at the top, with four legs sliding through slots, closing the flower when pulled down and opening it when pulled up. It gave us the motion we were after, but the legs kept slipping out of their slots. We sketched through several fixes on the whiteboard before redesigning the legs and cutting the slots directly into the base.

3.0 · Core mechanism 3.1 · Mechanism sketches

4.0 · The umbrella detour

We took a step back and tried building the skeleton from an umbrella frame with fabric stretched over it, closer in spirit to the fragility of a real flower. It looked more organic, but the frame couldn't take the strain of being pulled open and shut, and fabric wouldn't survive outdoors in wind. We dropped it in favor of something more robust.

4.0 · Umbrella + fabric

5.0 – 5.2 · The compact skeleton

Going back to iteration 2's idea, we redesigned it as two plates with slots riding on a servo-driven rail, small enough that we could drop the rail entirely once it proved stable. On top of that mechanism we added an ESP32 running four states (open, closed, and the transitions between), triggered by a single button, with NeoPixel LEDs cycling color as the flower opened and closed. Aluminum cans, cut and shaped into petals, gave us the reuse aesthetic fabric couldn't. The last step was a 3D-printed succulent, patterned after the Fibonacci sequence found throughout nature, used as a diffuser so the light glows through the center rather than just hanging below it.

Insight The 5.0 skeleton was the breakthrough: stable enough that we could drop the sliding rail entirely, and simple enough to layer electronics and, later, a diffuser on top of without redesigning the mechanism again.
Under the Hood

Wiring the movement and the light

An ESP32 drives the whole system. It's powered over USB, and a Wago connector splits that power out to the servo motor, the NeoPixel LED strip and the button module, each wired back into the ESP32 on its own pin (IO21, IO22 and IO33) to complete the circuit.

The Result

A flower that breathes

Aluminum-can petals close around a 3D-printed, Fibonacci-patterned succulent that diffuses the light from within.

We chose a succulent as the plant to model because its leaves grow in a pattern close to the Fibonacci sequence, the same sequence that shows up throughout nature, which gave the diffuser an organic visual logic beyond just looking plant-like. It also scatters the LED light more interestingly than a flat surface would, since light has to pass through many layers depending on the angle you view it from.

Reflection

What we'd do differently

The mechanism works, but it still reads as mechanical rather than organic. The next version aims for a softer transition.

Dourish (2001) distinguishes between two readings of embodiment: one where an object must physically manifest a phenomenon, and a looser one where it only needs to exist in real time and real space. Our lamp fits the looser definition more comfortably, since nature exists through it as a quality of movement rather than as a literal presence. Methodologically, we sketched constantly but never very rigorously: without something like Crazy 8's to structure the sketching sessions, some ideas (a weight-driven mechanism, in particular) kept resurfacing on paper without ever being built and tested. On materials, cans ultimately read as "reused" in a way fabric never did, even though fabric was easier to work with and looked more organic on its own.

Future direction Rebuilding the skeleton in aluminum with bolts, ball bearings and a lift-table-style leg mechanism instead of a continuous servo, aiming for smoother, less mechanical movement.
Outcome

Did it embody organic functionality and aesthetics?

Partly. The opening and closing motion still has a mechanical harshness to it, especially at the top and bottom of its range, but combined with the 3D-printed succulent it reads unmistakably as a flower, and when it's switched on, the motion feels closer to breathing than to machinery. The next version would aim to soften that transition and move to more durable materials and a faster motor, without losing the reused-aluminum aesthetic that gives the piece its point of view.
